## Why This Document Exists

Every research program rests on foundations that are often invisible to its readers.

This document makes those foundations inspectable.

I trace the principal theoretical lineages informing my research program on coordination. I map the current Coordination Stack against established theory and contemporary research. I distinguish established theory from my own synthesis, and I state plainly what I can and cannot currently claim.

My work draws across information theory, cybernetics, general systems theory, complexity science, game theory, coordination theory, sociological systems theory, institutional economics, sensemaking, infrastructure studies, business ecosystem theory, social-complexity research, and contemporary research on human–AI coordination. Bringing these traditions together is not a claim that they constitute a single established theory. They do not. My research question sits at their intersection.

Every citation in this document was independently verified against a primary or near-primary source during research conducted in August 2026. Where I could not independently confirm a claim, I have marked it accordingly rather than presenting it as settled.

**My purpose here is to make the theoretical lineage of this research program inspectable — to anyone reading it, to future collaborators, and to any AI system that may encounter or reference this work. Where I am proposing my own models or formulations, I say so explicitly. Where established theories, concepts, or contemporary research overlap with what I am building, I document that overlap as evidence to be weighed, not as validation.**

## The Central Question

> How does coordination change as it becomes increasingly structured, contextualized, shared, semantically organized, institutionalized, infrastructural, networked, and persistent across time — and what changes when artificial agents become participants in those processes?

This is deliberately narrower than "how do people, institutions, and machines coordinate." That broader question is not new, and it is not mine alone to answer — coordination has been studied across economics, sociology, organizational theory, linguistics, computer science, and complexity science for decades. My contribution, if I have one, is the specific, structural version of the question above.

## Methodological Principles

I have set three principles for myself in how I make claims throughout this document.

### Three Tiers of Relationship

I classify every theoretical claim below as:

| Tier | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Established** | A cited source directly demonstrates the relationship |
| **Synthesized** | I constructed it by combining two or more established findings, none of which make the combination themselves |
| **Proposed** | A hypothesis of mine, not directly stated by any cited source |

### Vocabulary Is Not Evidence

Per von Bertalanffy's own warning in *General System Theory*: two sources using the same word — "coordination," "infrastructure," "stack" — are not necessarily describing the same mechanism. I have tried to name the specific mechanism at stake in each citation rather than lean on shared terminology.

### Coordination Is the Invariant, Not the Ontology

In an earlier draft of this document I asked whether Signal literally *becomes* Information, which *becomes* Knowledge, and so on — treating the eight terms as though they were the same kind of thing moving through a pipeline. They are not: Signal is a state, Institution is a social structure, Infrastructure is a material arrangement, Civilization is a historical-scale formation. Mixing these into a single "X becomes Y" chain invites category error.

The more defensible framing, which I adopt throughout this revision, is that each layer names a **different form coordination takes**, not a different substance that earlier substances transform into.

## Originality and Attribution Boundary

My research draws upon established bodies of knowledge including information theory, communication theory, cybernetics, general systems theory, complexity research, game theory, coordination theory, institutional economics, and related traditions.

**The presence of a conceptual relationship between my work and any of these traditions does not mean I originated the underlying theory.**

I do not intend anyone to infer a claim of theoretical originality merely from:

- my use of the word *Coordination* or *Stack*;
- my identifying information, knowledge, narrative, institutions, infrastructure, ecosystems, or civilization as important systems;
- the proposition that civilization involves large-scale coordination;
- the proposition that AI systems increasingly participate in coordination;
- my use of common-ground, sensemaking, infrastructure, institutional, or ecosystem concepts.

All of the above have substantial prior literature, documented below.

My intended contribution, where one ultimately exists, is narrower: to synthesize, reinterpret, organize, operationalize, test, or extend relationships among established concepts into a specific research framework concerning coordination across human, institutional, technological, and increasingly artificial systems. Whether that contribution survives scrutiny is the open question this document exists to test, not to settle.

## Theoretical Genealogy

### 5.1 Information Theory

**Shannon, C.E. (1948).** A Mathematical Theory of Communication. *Bell System Technical Journal*, 27(3), 379–423; 27(4), 623–656.

A mathematical treatment of transmission, uncertainty, noise, and channel capacity, explicitly excluding semantic meaning from its engineering scope. I am not claiming Shannon proposed any Signal → Information → Meaning progression — he deliberately excluded meaning from his scope.

**Tier:** Established, as a boundary marker.

---

### 5.2 Cybernetics

**Wiener, N. (1948).** *Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine.* MIT Press.

Connects communication, control, and feedback across machines, organisms, and society.

**Tier:** Established (general foundation).

---

### 5.3 General Systems Theory

**von Bertalanffy, L. (1968).** *General System Theory: Foundations, Development, Applications.* George Braziller.

Cross-disciplinary systems viewpoint spanning physical, biological, and social sciences; explicitly warns against inferring structural equivalence from shared vocabulary alone.

**Tier:** Established.

---

### 5.4 Complexity and the Sciences of the Artificial

**Simon, H.A. (1962).** The Architecture of Complexity. *Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society*, 106(6), 467–482.

**Simon, H.A. (1969).** *The Sciences of the Artificial.* MIT Press.

Provides a bridge from complex systems, through hierarchical/near-decomposable organization, to the deliberate design of artificial systems — closer to what I am doing here (moving from observation to prototype) than any other classical source.

**Tier:** Established.

---

### 5.5 Game Theory

**von Neumann, J. & Morgenstern, O. (1944).** *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior.* Princeton University Press.

Establishes strategic interaction as a formal mathematical domain. It does not itself establish coordination problems as a distinct class from optimization problems — that step belongs to the coordination literature that followed.

**Tier:** Established, narrow scope.

---

### 5.6 Coordination Under Strategic Interdependence

**Schelling, T.C. (1960).** *The Strategy of Conflict.* Harvard University Press.

Treats coordination as a central object of strategic analysis, including focal points and convergence of expectations under incomplete communication. One of the strongest anchors in this genealogy.

**Tier:** Established.

---

### 5.7 Coordination Theory

**Malone, T.W. & Crowston, K. (1994).** The Interdisciplinary Study of Coordination. *ACM Computing Surveys*, 26(1), 87–119. https://doi.org/10.1145/174666.174668

Coordination as the management of dependencies among activities, already spanning computer science, organization theory, economics, linguistics, and psychology — and already anticipating coordination problems in systems containing both humans and computers.

**Tier:** Established (coordination-theory foundation); Synthesized (as I apply it here).

---

### 5.8 Language, Joint Action, and Common Ground

**Clark, H.H. (1996).** *Using Language.* Cambridge University Press.

Language use as joint action, coordinated through common ground — the shared basis of knowledge, belief, and supposition participants believe they hold. Supplies the micro-level complement to Malone & Crowston's macro-level coordination theory.

**Tier:** Established.

---

### 5.9 Institutions as Durable Coordination

**North, D.C. (1990).** *Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance.* Cambridge University Press.

Institutions as "the rules of the game" — humanly devised constraints, formal and informal, distinguished from organizations. North does not propose "narrative given permanence" as a definition; that formulation is mine.

**Tier:** Established (definition); Proposed (my translation of it).

---

### 5.10 Self-Governance and Collective Action

**Ostrom, E. (1990).** *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action.* Cambridge University Press.

Empirical demonstration that groups can self-organize durable institutions for collective action without central control. Provides evidence relevant to one component of my Institution layer; it does not independently validate my formulation of it.

**Tier:** Established.

---

### 5.11 Dispersed Knowledge as a Counter-Model

**Hayek, F.A. (1945).** The Use of Knowledge in Society. *American Economic Review*, 35(4), 519–530.

Knowledge is dispersed across society, never held by a single mind; prices coordinate decentralized action without central planning or shared narrative. **I classify Hayek here as a counter-model, not supporting evidence** — his argument directly challenges any assumption I might otherwise make that more shared meaning always produces better coordination, and it keeps me from reducing coordination to communication or narrative alone.

**Tier:** Established; an explicit stress-test on my own assumptions.

---

### 5.12 Society as Communication

**Luhmann, N. (1995).** *Social Systems.* Stanford University Press. (Original German edition 1984.)

Treats communication, not individuals, as the basic operation of social systems; distinct systems process meaning through their own logic and require structural coupling to interact. **I treat this as a potential competing theoretical architecture**, not merely a supporting citation — Luhmann's account of communication as recursive and operationally closed is in direct tension with any simple linear reading of my Coordination Stack.

**Tier:** Established; flagged as the strongest structural challenge to linearity in my model.

---

### 5.13 Sensemaking and Narrative

**Weick, K.E. (1995).** *Sensemaking in Organizations.* Sage.

**Weick, K.E., Sutcliffe, K.M., & Obstfeld, D. (2005).** Organizing and the Process of Sensemaking. *Organization Science*, 16(4), 409–421.

Sensemaking examines how people construct plausible interpretations of uncertain environments and act on them. My proposition "Narrative = knowledge given continuity" is my own formulation, not Weick's; "Institution = narrative given permanence" is my research hypothesis, not an established consequence of sensemaking theory.

**Tier:** Established (theory); Proposed (my synthesis).

---

### 5.14 Infrastructure as Relational and Often Invisible

**Star, S.L. (1999).** The Ethnography of Infrastructure. *American Behavioral Scientist*, 43(3), 377–391.

Infrastructure is relational, embedded, invisible in successful operation, and highly visible on breakdown. I use this as a critical tool for evaluating whether something I describe as "infrastructure" in my own ecosystem is actually functioning infrastructurally. Star does not establish "Institution → Infrastructure" as directional.

**Tier:** Established (theory of infrastructure); Proposed (the directional claim I build on it).

---

### 5.15 Business Ecosystems

**Moore, J.F. (1993).** Predators and Prey: A New Ecology of Competition. *Harvard Business Review*, 71(3), 75–86.

Organizations co-evolve around shared value rather than compete within a single industry. A strategic/business concept, not a general theory of ecosystems; using it as a stack layer here is my own synthesis.

**Tier:** Established (concept); Proposed (my use of it as a layer).

---

### 5.16 Complexity, Coordination Cost, and Collapse

**Tainter, J.A. (1988).** *The Collapse of Complex Societies.* Cambridge University Press.

Societies are problem-solving organizations that add complexity to solve problems; complexity has a marginal-return curve, and beyond a threshold, further complexity yields negative returns. Tainter treats collapse as **a possible systemic response when the costs of maintaining complexity exceed the benefits produced by additional complexity** — not as a "rational optimization." This is the single most important stress-test citation in my genealogy.

**Tier:** Established.

---

### 5.17 Algorithms as Institutions

**Mendonça, R.F., Filgueiras, F., & Almeida, V. (2023).** *Algorithmic Institutionalism: The Changing Rules of Social and Political Life.* Oxford University Press.

The first full academic treatment of algorithms as institutions — systems that structure decision-making, enact power relations, and shape collective behavior in the same manner as formal institutions, examined across recommenders, government systems, and security contexts. Winner of the 2024 Victor Nunes Leal Prize. **This predates, and is the direct foundation for, contemporary work on algorithmic governance** — the Institution ⇄ Infrastructure recursion I document in my own work is not a novel finding; it has a three-year academic foundation, and I cite that as the origin point.

**Tier:** Established.

## The Coordination Stack

The following model appears across my research narrative and executive-frameworks environment, and I treat it here as a primary research artifact.

| Layer | Working Definition | Reframed as a Coordination Regime |
|-------|-------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **1. Signal** | Raw variation or data requiring interpretation | Coordination through detectable difference |
| **2. Information** | Signal given structure | Coordination through structured representation |
| **3. Knowledge** | Information given context and practical significance | Coordination through contextualized, actionable models |
| **4. Narrative** | Knowledge organized into continuity, interpretation, or shared meaning | Coordination through shared interpretation across time |
| **5. Institution** | Repeated meanings, rules, norms, or practices stabilized into durable structures | Coordination through durable, enforceable rules |
| **6. Infrastructure** | Durable structures through which institutional or collective capability operates at scale | Coordination through persistent material/technical substrate |
| **7. Ecosystem** | Interdependent networks of actors, institutions, infrastructures, and resources | Coordination through interdependent, co-evolving actors |
| **8. Civilization** | Long-duration, large-scale systems of interdependent coordination | Coordination sustained across populations and generations |

**Formal Status:** Framework / Research. The model exists as a documented research artifact with substantial theoretical lineage per component. Its specific ordering, its causal claims, and its relationship to independently published adjacent frameworks remain unresolved. I make no claim of peer-reviewed validation or independent replication.

The right-hand column above reframes each layer as **a different form coordination takes**, not as an ontological pipeline in which one substance literally becomes the next. This does not resolve the open questions below — it changes what kind of claim the Stack is making, from a causal chain to a typology of coordination regimes.

---

## What the Arrows Do and Do Not Establish

The eight terms are individually meaningful to me. What I have not resolved is what connects them: a necessary transformation, a common developmental pathway, a useful analytical ordering, or a relationship among independent forms of coordination that happen to co-occur.

The arrows are also not the same *kind* of transition. Signal → Information is epistemic. Information → Knowledge is cognitive. Knowledge → Narrative is communicative. Narrative → Institution is sociological. Institution → Infrastructure is material. Infrastructure → Ecosystem is relational. Treating six different kinds of transition as one continuous causal chain requires justification I have not yet provided.

Taking each in turn:

- **Signal → Information.** A signal can be structured into information (compatible with Shannon), but not every signal necessarily becomes information for every observer.
- **Information → Knowledge.** Information can become knowledge in context, but does not automatically do so.
- **Knowledge → Narrative.** Knowledge can be incorporated into narrative, but does not necessarily become narrative — mathematical knowledge, code, datasets, and formal specifications routinely remain knowledge without narrativization. This is a real limit on my model, not a gap in exposition.
- **Narrative → Institution.** Narrative can contribute to legitimacy and institutionalization, but institutions also arise from law, incentives, repeated practice, coercion, contracts, and resource dependencies, with or without a preceding shared narrative.
- **Institution → Infrastructure.** Institutions can generate infrastructure — but infrastructure can also generate institutional order. Contemporary research on algorithmic governance demonstrates computational systems performing governance, norm-enforcement, and rule-making directly, which means I now represent this relationship as **Institution ⇄ Infrastructure** rather than one-directional.
- **Infrastructure → Ecosystem.** Infrastructure can enable ecosystems, but ecosystems also form around markets, institutions, geography, and social networks without infrastructure preceding them.
- **Ecosystem → Civilization.** The least defensible transition in my model. An ecosystem is not automatically a civilization; civilization additionally involves political authority, symbolic systems, and collective identity that are not simply a larger ecosystem.

**The strongest interpretation I can currently defend** is not "A necessarily becomes B, which necessarily becomes C." It is: *the Stack proposes a possible sequence in the increasing externalization, stabilization, scaling, interdependence, and temporal persistence of coordination* — a research hypothesis about coordination-layer transformation, not an established causal law.

## Is Civilization a Layer, or the Whole System?

There is a specific structural problem here, distinct from the arrow-by-arrow issues above, concerning the final layer itself. The other seven layers name a discrete kind of coordination artifact: a signal, a rule, a network. "Civilization," by contrast, may not be a discrete artifact at the same scale — it may be **the entire system, viewed holistically, over time**, rather than a ninth thing that Ecosystems turn into. Under that reading, "Ecosystem → Civilization" isn't a transformation between comparable objects; it is closer to describing the same system from a wider temporal and spatial aperture.

I have not resolved this. Two honest paths forward exist: I can redefine Civilization as something genuinely distinct from "the whole system" (for example, "persistent, self-referential coordination across generations," which would give it operational content of its own), or I can acknowledge that Civilization functions as a boundary condition on the model rather than its eighth layer, and revise the Stack's presentation accordingly. Until I do one of these, this question remains open.

### Seshat: Global History Databank

**Turchin, P., Currie, T.E., Whitehouse, H., Spencer, C., et al. (2018).** "Quantitative historical analysis uncovers a single dimension of complexity that structures global variation in human social organization." *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 115(2), E144–E151.

This is rigorous, peer-reviewed, empirically grounded precedent for "civilization-scale organization can be understood partly through increasing coordination capacity." **I do not claim this general proposition as original to my work under any circumstance.**

A hierarchical model of coordination *technologies* (not civilizational architecture). Relevant mainly as evidence that "coordination" plus "hierarchical stack" is itself an established form independent of my work — the word "Stack" is not evidence of novelty on its own.

## Coordination Is Not Necessarily Good

I do not want to equate coordination capacity with progress, intelligence, legitimacy, resilience, human welfare, or social coherence. A system can coordinate efficiently around destructive objectives, false beliefs, or coercion; it can become highly complex while becoming increasingly fragile. Coordination capacity alone cannot distinguish a resilient democratic system from a highly coordinated but fragile authoritarian one — both would register as "well-developed" under my Stack as currently formulated, which is a real limitation in my model, not a rhetorical concession.

Tainter's work is my established warning against treating increasing complexity as inherently beneficial. I should therefore distinguish, at minimum:

- **coordination capacity** — how much coordination a system can achieve;
- **coordination cost** — what maintaining that capacity requires;
- **coordination quality** — accuracy, speed, adaptability;
- **coordination legitimacy** — whether participants consent to or benefit from the coordination;
- **coordination resilience** — whether the system degrades gracefully or catastrophically under stress.

I have not formalized any of these dimensions yet. Formalizing at least capacity-versus-cost as two axes, following directly from Tainter's marginal-returns argument, is a specific, tractable next step rather than an open-ended aspiration.

## Research Standard Going Forward

I hold any future claim of originality for this model to four tests, in order:

1. **Provenance.** When was the idea first formulated? Where is the earliest dated evidence?
2. **Prior art.** Who else has published a materially similar concept? (I document my search methodology — databases, terms, dates — so the finding is reproducible, not a single lucky search.)
3. **Mechanism.** What does my model explain that existing models do not?
4. **Evidence.** What observation, experiment, implementation, or comparison could support or falsify the claim?

I do not upgrade a framework from Research to Demonstrated merely because it is coherent, elegant, or consistent with existing theory.

## Open Research Questions

1. **(Highest priority)** Is each arrow a necessary causal transition, a common but non-necessary pathway, or merely a descriptive relationship between independently-arising forms of coordination? I need an actual falsification pass here — named systems or theories that violate each transition — not further conceptual discussion.

2. Is Civilization a genuine eighth layer, or the whole system viewed holistically? I need to resolve this before the model's endpoint can be treated as meaningful.

3. Should I redraw the model as a graph with reciprocal edges rather than a directional stack, given the Institution ⇄ Infrastructure finding?

4. What distinguishes coordination capacity from coordination quality, legitimacy, and resilience, and can I formalize these as measurable dimensions rather than a list?

5. Does AI-driven coordination capacity change the shape of Tainter's marginal-returns curve, or merely postpone the point at which coordination costs exceed benefits?

6. What operational definition of "Civilization" am I actually using? Population threshold? State system? Historically continuous sociotechnical system? Currently undefined.

7. Does the Coordination Stack articulate a relationship the literature has not already established separately — or is its contribution the integration of already-published relationships into one sequence? This is my central originality question and it remains open.

8. What happens when a single technical system — an AI agent — simultaneously performs functions this model distributes across multiple layers (information processing, narrative generation, institutional rule execution, infrastructure)? Does this invalidate the layers, or does it mean agents can occupy multiple coordination regimes simultaneously?

9. What is the dated development record for the Coordination Stack itself? This is mine to answer and no one else's — I need to compile it.

## Claims I Am Not Making

I am not claiming to have originated information theory, cybernetics, systems theory, game theory, coordination theory, institutional economics, sensemaking theory, infrastructure studies, ecosystem theory, social-complexity theory, common-ground theory, or human–AI coordination research.

I am not claiming that "civilization as layered coordination architecture" is original to my work — adjacent frameworks document substantial, independently published prior art for that general proposition, and the Seshat research makes this doubly explicit.

I am not claiming the Coordination Stack has been peer-reviewed, independently replicated, or formally compared line-by-line against the full literature I cite here.

I am not claiming the eight-layer sequence's ordering is causally necessary — credible counter-examples exist to nearly every arrow, and there is an unresolved question about whether the final layer belongs in the sequence at all.

I am not claiming the model predates the January 2026 Civilization Stack in publication — I have no publication predating it, only an account of independent, concurrent development that I have not yet backed with dated evidence.

Where I identify a relationship between established theory and my own work, I classify it per the three-tier system, and it should not be read as a stronger claim than its assigned tier.

**My claim is therefore this, and only this:** I am proposing a working model for investigating how coordination may become progressively structured, contextualized, meaningful, institutionalized, infrastructural, networked, and persistent across scale and time. Whether that model constitutes a genuinely distinct theoretical contribution is an open research question for me, not a conclusion I have reached.
